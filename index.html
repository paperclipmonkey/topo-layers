3 Set the layer heights

Drag a marker to move a level, click open ground to add one, click a marker to remove it.

A doline only appears if a level falls between its floor and its spill point. Keep terrain smoothing low or shallow ones get flattened away before they can be found.

Range and exact heights
How the four spacings differ
Equal interval
The same height gain between every layer. The most faithful to the landscape, and the usual choice — a steep face gets tightly packed terraces and a flat plain gets almost none.
Equal area
Every layer covers roughly the same amount of the map. Use it when one height band swamps the piece — a big flat plain with one hill — and equal interval leaves you with one enormous plate and a few tiny ones.
Round numbers
Levels land on tidy heights: 100, 200, 300. Reads well when the piece is labelled, and matches a printed map. The number of layers follows from the range, so it may not be exactly the count you asked for.
Show depressions
Places levels so closed hollows — corries, craters, dolines — actually get a ring instead of vanishing between two layers. The emphasis slider trades the hollows off against the overall shape of the ground.
Doing it by hand
Whatever the rule picks, you can overrule it. Drag a marker to move a level, click open ground to add one, click a marker to remove it. Alt drags off the round-number snap; the arrow keys nudge the selected level and Delete drops it.
A bigger picking area
The bar below the plot drags the histogram taller, and so does the panel edge. Double-click the bar to put it back.

Cut quality

How the raw terrain is cleaned up into shapes a laser can cut well.

Min feature drops islands smaller than this — set it to 2–3× your material thickness. Kerf widens each part by half the beam width; leave it at 0 if your laser software already applies kerf offset.

What each control does
Terrain smoothing
Blurs the elevation data before the contours are traced. Raise it if the layers come out crinkly with the stair-steps of the source data; too much and shallow hollows flatten away before they can be found.
Curve smoothing
Rounds the traced outline itself. Makes for a nicer cut line without moving the contour much.
Simplify
Drops path nodes that sit within this distance of the line they are on. Smaller files and faster cuts; too high and outlines go visibly angular.
Min feature
Islands smaller than this are dropped. Set it to two or three times your material thickness — anything smaller is fragile, hard to glue, and likely to char away.
Min hole
The same, for holes cut inside a layer.
Kerf
The laser burns away a little material each side of the line. This widens every part by half that width so the pieces still fit. Leave it at 0 if your laser software already applies kerf offset — doing it twice makes everything oversized.

Place-name lettering
Names engraved
—

Names are engraved on whichever layer is the visible surface there, in a single-stroke font that cuts as centre lines. Set in capitals — far more readable engraved small. A name is first moved and shrunk to try to find clear ground; only if that fails does the straddle rule apply. Split lettering is cut at the plate edge and continues on the next plate down, so the name still reads across the step. Peaks are never filtered by settlement size.

Where detail gets engraved
Layer it sits on
Each river, road or name is engraved on whichever plate is the visible surface at that spot — so the detail follows the terrain up the stack.
Top layer only
Everything goes on the summit plate. Simplest to cut, but detail on lower ground disappears under the plates above.
Separate overlay sheet
All the detail on one extra sheet of its own, to sit over the finished stack or to cut from clear acrylic.
Names that straddle a step
A place name is moved and shrunk first to find clear ground. If it still crosses a plate edge, this decides what happens: split it across both plates so it still reads, leave it off, or engrave straight over the join.

Assembly aids

What ships alongside the cut parts to help you glue the stack up straight.

Holes are sited under the high ground first, deep inside the smallest layers, so one dowel carries as much of the stack as possible. Each sheet is only drilled where a hole clears its edges by the margin.

What each aid is for
Bottom layer is the full sheet
The base is cut as the whole rectangle rather than as a contour, giving the piece a square edge and something to build up from.
Engrave outline of the layer above
Each plate carries a faint line showing where the next one goes, so you can glue the stack up straight without measuring.
Alignment jig sheet
An extra sheet with every layer outline engraved on it, to drop each piece into place while the glue is wet.
Pin holes
Holes for alignment dowels, sited under the high ground first so one dowel carries as much of the stack as possible. A sheet is only drilled where the hole clears its own edges.

Four steps to a cuttable piece

  1. Choose the area. Search for a place or drag the frame over the map. Anything with hills works — coastlines cut beautifully too.
  2. Fetch the elevation. One click. The terrain heights come from an open, keyless dataset.
  3. Set the layer heights. Each layer is one sheet of material. Eight is a good start; drag the markers on the histogram to place them by hand.
  4. Build & download. You get one SVG per layer, plus the layers nested onto your stock boards and an alignment jig — all in one ZIP.

The panel shows one step at a time. The rail across the top takes you to any of them, in any order, and the footer moves you on once a step is settled. Everything optional — engraved rivers and roads, place names, your own GeoJSON points, dowel holes — sits inside the step it belongs to.

Wherever a group of options needs explaining there is a ? beside it. Open it: it says what each control actually does, in plain English.

Check it on the 3D tab before you cut — set your real material thickness there and see how much relief you actually get. It is usually less than people expect. Drag to turn it, shift-drag to pan, scroll to zoom right in on a plate edge.
